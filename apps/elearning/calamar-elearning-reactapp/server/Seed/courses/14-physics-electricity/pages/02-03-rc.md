# Charging and discharging capacitors

Connect an uncharged capacitor $C$ through a resistor $R$ to a battery $V$. At first the capacitor has no voltage, so the full battery voltage drives a current $V/R$. As charge builds up, the capacitor's voltage opposes the battery, and the current falls. By Kirchhoff's voltage law at any moment:

$$
V = IR + \frac{Q}{C}, \qquad I = \frac{dQ}{dt}
$$

The solution is exponential:

$$
V_C(t) = V\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad I(t) = \frac{V}{R}e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = RC
$$

The **time constant** $\tau = RC$ (ohms times farads gives seconds) sets the pace.

After one time constant the capacitor reaches $1 - e^{-1} = 63\,\%$ of the final voltage; after five, more than 99 %. Engineers treat it as "fully charged" after about $5\tau$.

**Discharging** through a resistor is the mirror image: $V_C(t) = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}$, falling to 37 % after one time constant.

## An energy puzzle

The battery pushes a total charge $Q = CV$ through a voltage $V$, so it supplies energy $QV = CV^2$. But the capacitor ends up holding only $\frac12 CV^2$. The other half is turned into heat in the resistor, and surprisingly, **whatever the value of $R$**: a smaller resistor means a larger current for a shorter time, with the same total heat. You can check: $\int I^2 R\, dt = \frac{V^2}{R}\int_0^\infty e^{-2t/RC}\,dt = \frac{V^2}{R} \cdot \frac{RC}{2} = \frac12 CV^2$.

## Where RC circuits are used

- **Timing**: the time constant sets the delay of a timer, the blink rate of an LED, the frequency of an oscillator (the classic 555 timer chip works this way).
- **Smoothing**: a large capacitor after a rectifier fills the dips in the voltage of a power supply.
- **Filtering**: an RC circuit passes slow signals and blocks fast ones, or the reverse; the AC chapter treats this as a frequency response.
- **Flash and pulse circuits**: charge slowly, discharge quickly.
- **Memory**: each bit of a DRAM chip is a tiny capacitor, which leaks its charge through unavoidable resistance in tens of milliseconds, so the chip refreshes every bit many times a second.

Your nerve cells behave like RC circuits too: the membrane is a capacitor (about 1 µF per cm²) with ion channels as resistors, and its time constant, typically milliseconds, sets how fast a neuron responds.
