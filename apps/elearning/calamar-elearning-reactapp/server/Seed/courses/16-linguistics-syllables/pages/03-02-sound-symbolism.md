# Sound symbolism: bouba, kiki and small i

## Bouba and kiki

Look at two shapes: one round and blobby, one sharp and spiky. Which one is called **bouba** and which **kiki**?

Almost everyone says the round one is *bouba* and the spiky one *kiki*. The psychologist Wolfgang Köhler first described the effect in 1929, with the invented words *maluma* and *takete*. In 2001, Vilayanur Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard renamed them *bouba* and *kiki* and found that most of their participants matched them the same way. In 2022, a study by Aleksandra Ćwiek and colleagues tested speakers of 25 languages, written in 10 different scripts: overall, about 70 % of answers matched *bouba* with the round shape, and the effect appeared in most (though not all) of the languages.

Why? The round shape matches rounded lips ([u], [o]) and the smooth, voiced [b]; the spiky shape matches spread lips ([i]) and the abrupt, voiceless [k]. The mapping links what the mouth does to what the eye sees.

## Small i

In 1929 Edward Sapir asked people which of two invented words, *mil* and *mal*, named a bigger table. About 80 % chose *mal*. High front vowels like [i] tend to suggest **smallness**, and open vowels like [a] **largeness**:

- English *teeny*, *wee*, *itsy-bitsy*, *little*; Spanish *chiquito*; Italian *piccolo*; French *petit*;
- many languages make diminutives with an [i]: Spanish *-ito*, Italian *-ino*, English *-y/-ie* (*doggy*, *Annie*), Romanian *-iță* and *-ică* (*fetiță* "little girl").

One explanation is the **frequency code** (John Ohala, 1984): small animals and small vocal tracts make high-pitched sounds, large ones low sounds, and [i] has the highest resonances of any vowel. It is a tendency, not a rule: English *big* has an [ɪ], and *small* has an open vowel.

## A worldwide test

In 2016 Damián Blasi and colleagues analyzed word lists for about 100 basic meanings, from more than 6,000 languages and dialects covering nearly two-thirds of the world's languages. Controlling for shared ancestry and geography, they still found associations between some meanings and some sounds, far beyond chance. Among them:

- words for **nose** tend to contain [n];
- words for **tongue** tend to contain [l];
- words for **small** tend to contain [i];
- words for **red** and **round** tend to contain [r].

Most meanings show no such bias: the sign is mostly arbitrary, as Saussure said. But not completely.

## Ideophones

Some languages have a whole class of sound-symbolic words, called **ideophones**, that depict sensations, movements and textures. Japanese has thousands, used constantly in everyday speech:

| Japanese | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *kira-kira* | sparkling, glittering |
| *doki-doki* | heart pounding (with excitement or fear) |
| *goro-goro* | rumbling (thunder); lazing around |
| *pika-pika* | shiny, flashing (the source of Pikachu's name) |
| *shiin* | dead silence |

Korean, many African languages (Yoruba, Ewe, Zulu) and many languages of South and Southeast Asia have rich ideophone systems too. They often reduplicate (repeat) a syllable, and patterns are consistent within each language: in Japanese, voiced consonants often suggest something bigger, heavier or rougher (*goro-goro*, a heavy rumble) than their voiceless partners (*koro-koro*, a light rolling).

## Phonaesthemes

English has smaller clusters: sounds shared by groups of words with related meanings, called **phonaesthemes**.

- *gl-* and light: *glow, gleam, glitter, glimmer, glint, glare, glisten*;
- *sn-* and the nose: *snout, sniff, sneeze, snore, snort, snot, snuffle*;
- *-ump* and heavy masses: *lump, bump, hump, clump, stump, plump*.

They are partly historical accident (one word spreading its pattern to others) and partly sound symbolism, and they show that speakers notice and extend patterns.
