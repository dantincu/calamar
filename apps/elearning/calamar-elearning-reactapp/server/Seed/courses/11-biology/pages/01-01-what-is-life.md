# What is life?

There is no one-line definition of life that every biologist accepts. Instead, living things share a set of properties. Each one alone also occurs in non-living things (a flame "grows" and "feeds"), but only life has all of them together.

## The characteristics of life

| Property | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cells** | All living things are made of one or more cells | a bacterium is one cell; you are about 37 trillion |
| **Metabolism** | They take in energy and matter and transform them | you burn glucose; a leaf captures light |
| **Homeostasis** | They keep their internal conditions stable | your body stays near 37 °C in winter and summer |
| **Growth and development** | They grow and change following internal instructions | a seed becomes a tree |
| **Reproduction** | They make more of their kind | bacteria divide; animals mate |
| **Heredity** | Offspring inherit instructions, in DNA | children resemble their parents |
| **Response** | They sense and react to their surroundings | a plant bends toward light |
| **Evolution** | Populations change over generations, adapting to their environment | bacteria become resistant to antibiotics |

**Viruses** sit on the border. They have genes and they evolve, but they have no metabolism of their own and can reproduce only inside a host cell. Most biologists don't count them as alive, but they study them as part of biology.

## Levels of organization

Biology works at every scale, and each level has properties that the level below doesn't have:

```
atom -> molecule -> organelle -> cell -> tissue -> organ -> organ system -> organism
     -> population -> community -> ecosystem -> biosphere
```

A single water molecule isn't wet, a single neuron doesn't think, and a single tree isn't a forest. These **emergent properties** are why biology needs its own methods, besides chemistry and physics.

## The scale of life

Sizes span about eleven orders of magnitude:

| Thing | Typical size |
|---|---|
| a protein | 5 nm |
| a virus | 20–300 nm |
| a bacterium (*E. coli*) | 2 µm long |
| a human cell | 10–30 µm |
| a human egg cell | 0.1 mm, just visible |
| a blue whale | 30 m |
| a giant sequoia | 85 m tall |

with $1\ \mu\text{m} = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$ and $1\ \text{nm} = 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$. A useful habit: when you meet a structure, ask how big it is, and how that size compares with the others.

## The unity and the diversity of life

About 2 million species have been named, and estimates of the total run to many millions more, most of them insects, fungi and microbes. Yet all of them:

- store their genes in **DNA** and read them with the **same genetic code** (with a few small variations);
- make proteins from the **same 20 amino acids**;
- use **ATP** as their energy currency;
- are built from cells bounded by a **lipid membrane**.

This shared machinery is the strongest evidence that all life descends from a common ancestor, a theme we return to in the chapter on evolution.

Living things are classified into three **domains**:

- **Bacteria**: single-celled, no nucleus.
- **Archaea**: single-celled, no nucleus, but chemically and genetically distinct from bacteria; many live in extreme environments.
- **Eukarya**: cells with a nucleus. They include protists, fungi, plants and animals.
