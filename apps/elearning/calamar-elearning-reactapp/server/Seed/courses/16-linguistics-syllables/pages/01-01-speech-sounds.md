# Speech sounds and the IPA

## How speech is made

Speech is breath, shaped. Air from the lungs passes the **vocal folds** in the larynx, which either vibrate (making **voiced** sounds, like [z] or any vowel) or stay apart (**voiceless** sounds, like [s]). Put your fingers on your throat and say "zzzz", then "ssss": you can feel the difference. Above the larynx, the tongue, lips, teeth, palate and nasal passage shape the sound.

## Consonants

Consonants obstruct the airflow somewhere. Phoneticians describe each by three properties:

- **place**: where the obstruction is: both lips (bilabial: [p b m]), teeth ridge (alveolar: [t d n s l]), soft palate (velar: [k g ŋ]), and others;
- **manner**: how much: a full stop and release (**stops**: [p t k b d g]), a narrow gap with turbulent air (**fricatives**: [f s ʃ v z]), airflow through the nose (**nasals**: [m n ŋ]), or air flowing around the tongue (**liquids**: [l r]);
- **voicing**: voiced or voiceless. [p] and [b] differ only in voicing.

So [m] is a voiced bilabial nasal, [k] a voiceless velar stop. The world's languages have stranger sounds too: the **clicks** of Zulu, Xhosa and the Khoisan languages of southern Africa; the **ejectives** of Georgian and Amharic, made with a burst of air from the closed throat; the **retroflex** consonants of Hindi, Tamil and Mandarin, with the tongue tip curled back.

## Vowels

Vowels let the air flow freely; their quality comes from the shape of the mouth. They are described by:

- **height** of the tongue: high [i u], mid [e o], low [a];
- **backness**: front [i e], central [ə], back [u o];
- **rounding** of the lips: [u] and [o] are rounded in most languages; French and German have rounded front vowels, like French *tu* [ty] and German *über* [y].

The five vowels [a e i o u] are the most common vowel system in the world's languages: Spanish, Japanese, Swahili, Greek and many others use them. English has many more, about 20 in standard British English, counting diphthongs.

## The International Phonetic Alphabet

Spelling is a poor guide to sound. English *ough* is pronounced differently in *though, through, tough, cough* and *bough*. The **International Phonetic Alphabet** (IPA), created in 1888 and still maintained by the International Phonetic Association, gives one symbol per sound, for every language. Transcriptions go in square brackets: *though* [ðəʊ], *through* [θɹuː], *tough* [tʌf].

A few symbols used in this course:

| IPA | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| [ʃ] | sh | English *ship*, Romanian *și* |
| [ŋ] | ng | English *sing*, Mandarin *zhōng* |
| [ə] | the neutral "uh" (schwa) | English *about*, Romanian *mă* |
| [ɨ] | a high central vowel | Romanian *î/â*, Russian *ы* |
| [ʔ] | glottal stop | the catch in English *uh-oh* |
| [ɾ] | a single tap of the tongue | Spanish *pero*, Japanese *r* |
| [◌̃] | nasal vowel | French *bon* [bɔ̃], Hindi *mā̃* |
| [ʰ] | a puff of air (aspiration) | English *pin* [pʰɪn] |

## Phonemes: the sounds that matter

Not every difference in sound matters in every language. Hold your hand in front of your mouth and say *pin* and *spin*: the *p* of *pin* comes with a puff of air, [pʰ], the *p* of *spin* doesn't, [p]. English speakers hear them as the same sound, because swapping them never changes a word. They are two variants (**allophones**) of one **phoneme**, /p/ (phonemes go in slashes).

In Hindi, the same two sounds are separate phonemes, written with different letters: प [p] and फ [pʰ]. *pal* (पल) means "moment", and *phal* (फल) means "fruit". A Hindi speaker hears the difference as clearly as an English speaker hears *pat* and *bat*.

Each language cuts the space of possible sounds differently:

- Japanese has one liquid, a tap [ɾ], where English has two phonemes, /l/ and /r/: so *rice* and *lice* are hard to tell apart for many Japanese learners of English.
- Standard Arabic has no /p/: its speakers often pronounce *Pepsi* as *Bebsi*.
- Spanish /b/ and /v/ are one phoneme: *vaca* and *baca* sound the same.

Phoneme inventories vary widely. Rotokas, in Papua New Guinea, has about 11 phonemes; Hawaiian 13; English around 44; and Taa, in Botswana, well over 100, most of them clicks.
