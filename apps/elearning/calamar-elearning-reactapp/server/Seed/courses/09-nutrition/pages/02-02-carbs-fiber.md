# Carbohydrates and fiber

Carbohydrates are the body's main fuel, and the brain's preferred one. They come in three kinds:

- **Sugars**: glucose, fructose, sucrose (table sugar), lactose (milk sugar).
- **Starches**: long chains of glucose, in grains, potatoes and legumes.
- **Fiber**: carbohydrates that human enzymes can't digest. The bacteria in the gut ferment some of them, which is why fiber still provides about 2 kcal/g.

## Free sugars

The WHO separates the sugars in whole fruit, vegetables and milk from **free sugars**: sugars added to food, plus those in honey, syrups and fruit juice. Only free sugars are linked to tooth decay and weight gain. The guideline:

$$
\text{free sugars} < 10\,\%\ \text{of energy} \quad (\text{ideally} < 5\,\%)
$$

At 2000 kcal, 10 % is $0.10 \times 2000 / 4 = 50$ g, about 12 teaspoons. A 500 ml bottle of a typical soft drink (10.6 g/100 ml) has 53 g: the whole day's allowance in one bottle.

An EU label's "of which sugars" counts **all** sugars, including lactose and fruit sugar. Plain milk shows 4.7 g of sugars per 100 ml, none of it added; check the ingredient list to see whether sugar was added.

## Starches and the glycemic index

The **glycemic index** (GI) ranks foods by how fast they raise blood glucose, compared with pure glucose (GI 100). It depends on the food, and on how it is prepared: pasta cooked *al dente* has a lower GI than soft pasta, and cooled potatoes lower than hot ones.

GI ignores *how much* carbohydrate you eat, so the **glycemic load** is more useful:

$$
\text{GL} = \frac{\text{GI} \times \text{available carbohydrate (g)}}{100}
$$

A GL of 10 or less is low, 20 or more is high. Watermelon has a high GI (about 72), but a 150 g slice has only about 11 g of carbohydrate:

$$
\text{GL} = \frac{72 \times 11}{100} \approx 8 \quad \text{(low)}
$$

Practical rules follow from this: eat whole grains and legumes, combine carbohydrates with protein, fat and vegetables, which slow digestion, and don't fear fruit.

## Fiber

Fiber feeds the gut bacteria, keeps you regular, slows the absorption of sugar, lowers LDL cholesterol, and helps you feel full. Higher fiber intakes are consistently linked to lower rates of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colon cancer.

EFSA's adequate intake is **25 g/day**; many Europeans eat around 20 g. Where to find it (per 100 g, dry or raw):

| Food | Fiber (g) |
|---|---|
| dry white beans | 15.2 |
| dry chickpeas | 12.2 |
| dry lentils | 10.7 |
| oats | 10.1 |
| sunflower seeds | 8.6 |
| wholemeal bread | 7 |
| frozen peas | 4.5 |
| carrots, beetroot | 2.8 |
| cabbage | 2.5 |
| apple | 2.4 |
| white bread | 2.7 |
| white rice | 1.3 |

A bowl of oats in the morning (60 g: 6 g), a lentil soup at lunch (70 g dry: 7.5 g), and a few portions of vegetables and fruit (about 10 g) reach 25 g easily.

Increase fiber **gradually** over a couple of weeks, and drink enough water, or expect bloating.

## Whole grains

A whole grain keeps the bran and the germ, where most of the fiber, vitamins and minerals are. In Romanian shops look for *integral* (wholemeal) bread and flour, and read the ingredients: "brown" bread is sometimes white bread colored with malt. Oats are always whole grain.
