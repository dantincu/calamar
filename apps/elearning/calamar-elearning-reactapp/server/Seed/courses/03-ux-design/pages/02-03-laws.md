# Fitts's and Hick's laws

Two results from experimental psychology turn common UX advice ("make buttons big", "keep menus short") into formulas.

## Fitts's law: pointing

The time to move a pointer onto a target grows with the distance $D$ to the target and shrinks with its width $W$ (measured along the direction of movement). In the Shannon form used in HCI:

$$
MT = a + b \cdot \log_2\!\left(\frac{D}{W} + 1\right)
$$

- $MT$ is the movement time.
- $a$ and $b$ are constants fitted for a device and a person, roughly $a \approx 0.1\,\text{s}$ and $b \approx 0.15\,\text{s/bit}$ for a mouse.
- The logarithm is the **index of difficulty**, $ID = \log_2(D/W + 1)$, in bits.

**Worked example.** A 32 px button 600 px away has $ID = \log_2(600/32 + 1) = \log_2(19.75) \approx 4.30$ bits, so $MT \approx 0.1 + 0.15 \times 4.30 \approx 0.75\,\text{s}$. Doubling the width to 64 px gives $ID \approx 3.38$ bits and $MT \approx 0.61\,\text{s}$, about 19% faster.

Because of the logarithm, **doubling the size removes about one bit**, whatever the distance. Consequences:

- Make frequent and primary actions larger.
- Put related controls close together.
- Screen edges and corners behave as if they were infinitely deep, because the pointer stops there. That's why menu bars at the top edge are easy to hit.
- On touch screens, aim for targets of at least 44×44 CSS px. WCAG 2.2 requires at least 24×24 (criterion 2.5.8).

## Hick's law: choosing

The time to choose among $n$ equally likely options grows with the logarithm of $n$:

$$
RT = a + b \cdot \log_2(n + 1)
$$

Going from 4 to 16 options adds $b \cdot (\log_2 17 - \log_2 5) \approx 1.77\,b$, about a quarter of a second with $b = 0.15\,\text{s}$. Because the growth is logarithmic, grouping pays off: a menu of 16 items in 4 labeled groups is faster to scan than one undivided list.

Hick's law applies to *unfamiliar* choices that people have to read. Experts who know where an item is just point at it, and then Fitts's law decides.
