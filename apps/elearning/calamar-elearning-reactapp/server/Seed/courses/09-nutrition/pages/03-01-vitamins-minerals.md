# Vitamins and minerals

There are 13 vitamins and about 15 essential minerals. You don't need to track them all: if you eat vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and some dairy, eggs or fish, most are covered. These are the exceptions worth knowing about.

## The ones that often run short

| Nutrient | Adult reference (EFSA) | Why it runs short | Good sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin D** | 15 µg/day | Few foods contain it; the skin makes it from sunlight, but at Bucharest's latitude (44° N) winter sun is too weak, roughly from November to February | oily fish, eggs, fortified foods; a supplement in winter |
| **Iron** | 11 mg men, 16 mg women before menopause | Losses through menstruation; plant iron is poorly absorbed | liver, red meat; legumes, oats, seeds and spinach with vitamin C |
| **Vitamin B12** | 4 µg/day | Only in animal foods | meat, fish, eggs, dairy; **a supplement for vegans** |
| **Iodine** | 150 µg/day | Little in soil and water in many regions, including much of Romania | **iodized salt**, fish, dairy |
| **Calcium** | 950 mg/day (1000 mg at 18–24) | Low dairy intake | milk, yogurt, cheese; fortified plant drinks; tofu set with calcium; sesame, kale |
| **Folate** | 330 µg/day; 400 µg folic acid before and in early pregnancy | Low vegetable intake | leafy greens, legumes, citrus |

## Vitamin D

Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium, and for bones and muscles. Few foods contain much: 100 g of mackerel has about 15 µg, an egg about 1–2 µg.

In summer, 10–20 minutes of midday sun on the arms and face several times a week lets most light-skinned people make enough. In winter at Bucharest's latitude the sun is too low, and blood levels typically fall by spring. Many European health authorities advise a **10 µg (400 IU) supplement in autumn and winter**; ask a doctor about more, or about a blood test.

$$
1\ \mu\text{g} = 40\ \text{IU} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 10\ \mu\text{g} = 400\ \text{IU}, \quad 25\ \mu\text{g} = 1000\ \text{IU}
$$

## Iron, and how to absorb more of it

Iron comes in two forms:

- **Heme iron**, in meat and fish: about 15–35 % is absorbed.
- **Non-heme iron**, in plants and eggs: typically 2–20 % is absorbed, depending on the rest of the meal.

So the **amount absorbed** is what counts:

$$
\text{absorbed} = \text{iron in the food} \times \text{absorption fraction}
$$

You can raise the fraction for plant iron:

- **Vitamin C in the same meal** (peppers, tomatoes, sauerkraut, citrus, parsley) can multiply absorption several times.
- **Tea and coffee** with the meal reduce it, so drink them an hour before or after.
- Calcium in large amounts also reduces it a little.

Lentils with tomatoes and peppers, or beans with sauerkraut salad, are good traditional combinations.

Don't take iron supplements without a blood test: too much iron is harmful too.

## Iodine and salt

The thyroid needs iodine. Romania's soil is low in it, especially in the hills and mountains, and goiter used to be common. Since 2002 Romanian law requires salt for households and bakeries to be iodized. If you use salt, use **iodized** salt (*sare iodată*). Sea salt and "Himalayan" salt are usually not iodized, and have no health advantage.

## Supplements

For healthy adults with a varied diet, the evidence supports few supplements:

- **Vitamin D** in winter (see above).
- **Folic acid**, 400 µg/day, for anyone who could become pregnant.
- **Vitamin B12** for vegans (and many people over 50, who absorb it less well).
- **Iron** only when a blood test shows a deficiency.

Multivitamins don't prevent heart disease or cancer in well-nourished people. Megadoses can harm: vitamin A in pregnancy, vitamin B6 (nerve damage), vitamin D at very high doses. "Natural" doesn't mean safe.
