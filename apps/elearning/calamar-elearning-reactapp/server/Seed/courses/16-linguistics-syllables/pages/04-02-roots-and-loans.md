# Semitic roots and travelling words

## Roots made of consonants

The Semitic languages (Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Aramaic, Maltese and others) build words in a remarkable way. Most words come from a **root** of three consonants that carries a core meaning, slotted into **patterns** of vowels and extra consonants that carry grammar. From the Arabic root **k-t-b**, "writing":

| Word | Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *kataba* | CaCaCa | he wrote |
| *yaktubu* | yaCCuCu | he writes |
| *kitāb* | CiCāC | book |
| *kātib* | CāCiC | writer, clerk |
| *maktab* | maCCaC | office, desk |
| *maktaba* | maCCaCa | library, bookshop |
| *maktūb* | maCCūC | written; a letter |

The pattern *maCCaC* means "place of": *maktab* is the place of writing, and with the root **d-r-s** ("study"), *madrasa* is the place of studying, a school, and *dars* is a lesson.

From **s-l-m**, "peace, wholeness": *salām* "peace" (as in the greeting *as-salāmu ʿalaykum*), *islām* "submission (to God)", *muslim* "one who submits". Hebrew, a sister language, has the same root: *shalom* "peace". Hebrew *k-t-v* (the *b* softened to *v*) gives *katav* "he wrote" and *mikhtav* "letter".

In these languages, then, the syllable is not the natural unit of meaning: the consonant root is. The same three consonants appear in different syllables in every word of the family. Writing reflects this: the Arabic and Hebrew scripts are **abjads**, which write mainly the consonants, leaving most vowels for the reader to supply.

## Words that travel: the book

Words follow religion, trade and empire. Arabic *kitāb* spread with Islam and Arabic learning: Persian *ketāb*, Turkish *kitap*, Hindi and Urdu *kitāb*, Swahili *kitabu* (with a final vowel added, as Swahili syllables must be open), Malay and Indonesian *kitab* (a religious book). A root from one family became an everyday word in at least five others.

## Tea: te and cha

Almost every language calls tea either something like **te** or something like **cha**, and the split is a map of history.

- **cha** comes from Mandarin and Cantonese *chá* 茶. It spread overland along the Silk Road and by Portuguese traders, who bought tea through Macau: Persian *chāy*, Hindi and Urdu *chāy*, Arabic *shāy*, Turkish *çay*, Russian *chay*, Swahili *chai*, Portuguese *chá*, Japanese and Korean *cha*. Romanian *ceai* comes through Russian.
- **te** comes from the Min Nan dialect of the Fujian coast, *tê*, where the Dutch bought their tea in the 17th century and shipped it to Europe by sea: Dutch *thee*, English *tea*, French *thé*, Spanish *té*, German *Tee*, Italian *tè*, Hebrew *te*, Indonesian *teh*.

The *World Atlas of Language Structures* maps this (chapter 138, by Östen Dahl): the *te* languages cluster along the old sea routes, the *cha* languages along the land routes. One syllable, two journeys.

## Chinese syllables in Japan, Korea and Vietnam

For centuries, Chinese was the language of learning in East Asia, and Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese borrowed thousands of words with Chinese characters, adapting each syllable to their own sound systems. The borrowed readings are called **Sino-Japanese** (the *on'yomi*), **Sino-Korean** and **Sino-Vietnamese**:

| Character | Meaning | Mandarin | Japanese (on) | Korean | Vietnamese |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 馬 | horse | mǎ | ba | ma | mã |
| 山 | mountain | shān | san | san | sơn |
| 學 / 学 | study | xué | gaku | hak | học |
| 國 / 国 | country | guó | koku | guk | quốc |
| 大 | big | dà | dai | dae | đại |

The borrowed forms preserve older Chinese pronunciations: the final *-k* of Korean *hak* and Vietnamese *học* was lost in Mandarin but survives in Cantonese (*hok6*). Korean *Hanguk* "Korea" contains the *guk* "country" of the table; Japanese *gakkō* "school" contains *gaku* "study". And Vietnamese *mã* "horse", from the world tour, is in the first row.

## Layers in one language: Romanian

Romanian, a Romance language surrounded by Slavic, Hungarian and Greek neighbors, shows its history in its basic syllables:

- inherited from Latin: *nu* "no" (Latin *nōn*), *la* "at" (Latin *illac* "that way"), *mi* "to me" (Latin *mihi*), *și* "and" (Latin *sīc*);
- borrowed from Slavic: *da* "yes", and many other everyday words such as *a iubi* "to love" and *prieten* "friend".

Even *yes* and *no*, two of the most basic words of any language, came from different sources.
