# Periodic trends

The configurations explain why properties change smoothly across the table. Two effects compete:

- moving **right** across a period, the nucleus gains protons, while the new electrons go into the same shell and screen each other poorly. The **effective nuclear charge** felt by the outer electrons grows, and pulls them closer;
- moving **down** a group, each period adds a new shell, farther from the nucleus and screened by all the inner shells.

## Atomic radius

Atoms get **smaller to the right** and **larger going down**. Sodium (about 186 pm, as the metal) is much larger than chlorine (about 99 pm) in the same period; cesium is the largest of the stable atoms.

Ions differ from their atoms: a cation is smaller than its atom (it lost its outer shell, and the remaining electrons are pulled in more strongly), an anion larger.

## Ionization energy

The **first ionization energy** is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom:

$$
\text{X}(g) \longrightarrow \text{X}^+(g) + e^-
$$

| Element | kJ/mol | | Element | kJ/mol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1312 | | Li | 520 |
| He | 2372 | | Na | 496 |
| N | 1402 | | K | 419 |
| O | 1314 | | F | 1681 |
| Ne | 2081 | | Ar | 1521 |

It **rises to the right** (electrons held more tightly) and **falls going down** (outer electrons farther away). Noble gases have the highest values, which is why they are unreactive; alkali metals the lowest, which is why they react so violently with water. Potassium in water, with 419 kJ/mol, is more violent than lithium, with 520.

Look at nitrogen and oxygen: oxygen is lower, against the trend. Nitrogen's three 2p electrons are each alone in an orbital; oxygen's fourth must pair up with another, and the repulsion between the two makes it easier to remove. Small exceptions like this are evidence for the orbital model.

**Successive** ionization energies tell you how many valence electrons an atom has. For sodium, the first is 496 kJ/mol and the second 4,562: removing a second electron means breaking into the full inner shell. Magnesium has a jump after the second, aluminium after the third.

## Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** (EN) measures how strongly an atom attracts the electrons in a bond. On Linus Pauling's scale, it runs from about 0.8 (cesium, potassium) to 3.98 (fluorine):

| | 1 | 2 | 13 | 14 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| period 2 | Li 0.98 | Be 1.57 | B 2.04 | C 2.55 | N 3.04 | O 3.44 | F 3.98 |
| period 3 | Na 0.93 | Mg 1.31 | Al 1.61 | Si 1.90 | P 2.19 | S 2.58 | Cl 3.16 |

It **rises to the right and going up**: fluorine, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine are the most electronegative elements. The noble gases usually have no value, because they rarely form bonds. The difference in EN between two atoms predicts the kind of bond they form, the subject of the next chapter.

## Metals and non-metals

The trends divide the table:

- **metals** (left and center, about three-quarters of the elements) have low ionization energies and electronegativities: they lose electrons easily, conduct electricity and form cations;
- **non-metals** (upper right) gain or share electrons;
- **metalloids** along the staircase between them (B, Si, Ge, As…) are in between. Silicon's in-between conductivity is the basis of all electronics.
