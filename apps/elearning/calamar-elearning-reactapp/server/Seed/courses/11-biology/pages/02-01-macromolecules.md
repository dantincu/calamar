# Water and the four macromolecules

About 96 % of your body mass is four elements: **oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen**. Carbon is the backbone of biology because each carbon atom forms four stable bonds, so it builds chains, rings and branches of almost any shape.

## Water

Cells are about 70 % water, and its odd properties make life possible. A water molecule is **polar**: the oxygen pulls electrons and carries a partial negative charge, the hydrogens a partial positive one. Neighboring molecules attract each other through **hydrogen bonds**. As a result:

- water **dissolves** ions and polar molecules (sugars, amino acids), so it is the medium of cell chemistry;
- it has a high **specific heat** ($4.18\ \text{J g}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$), so bodies and lakes change temperature slowly;
- **evaporating** it takes a lot of energy, about 2.4 kJ per gram at body temperature, which is why sweating cools you;
- ice is **less dense** than liquid water and floats, so lakes freeze from the top and life survives below;
- water molecules stick to each other and to surfaces (**cohesion and adhesion**), helping water rise in plants.

Non-polar molecules such as oils don't dissolve in water: they are **hydrophobic**. This simple fact shapes membranes and proteins.

## The four macromolecules

Most biological molecules are **polymers**: long chains of small units (**monomers**) joined by removing a water molecule (**condensation**), and split by adding one back (**hydrolysis**, which is what digestion does).

| Class | Monomer | Examples | Main roles |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | monosaccharides (glucose) | starch, glycogen, cellulose | fuel, energy storage, structure |
| Lipids | (not true polymers) fatty acids, glycerol | fats, phospholipids, steroids | energy storage, membranes, hormones |
| Proteins | 20 amino acids | enzymes, collagen, hemoglobin, antibodies | almost every job in the cell |
| Nucleic acids | nucleotides | DNA, RNA | storing and expressing genetic information |

### Carbohydrates

Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) is the main fuel of cells. Linked into chains, it gives very different materials depending only on the bond between the units:

- **starch** (plants) and **glycogen** (animals, in liver and muscle) are fuel stores that enzymes break down easily;
- **cellulose**, the most abundant organic molecule on Earth, forms the plant cell wall; humans have no enzyme to digest it, so for us it is dietary fiber.

### Lipids

**Fats** (triglycerides: glycerol plus three fatty acids) store about 9 kcal per gram, more than twice as much as carbohydrates, which is why animals store energy as fat. **Phospholipids** have a water-loving head and two water-fearing tails. In water they line up spontaneously into a double layer, the **lipid bilayer**, with the tails inside: this is the basis of every cell membrane. **Steroids** such as cholesterol and the sex hormones are lipids built on four rings.

### Proteins

A protein is a chain of amino acids, typically a few hundred long, linked by **peptide bonds**. Each of the 20 amino acids has a different side chain: charged, polar, hydrophobic, small or bulky. The sequence determines how the chain folds:

1. **primary structure**: the sequence of amino acids;
2. **secondary**: local coils (α-helices) and pleats (β-sheets), held by hydrogen bonds;
3. **tertiary**: the overall 3D shape, with hydrophobic side chains buried inside;
4. **quaternary**: several chains together (hemoglobin has four).

Shape is function. Heat or acid can unfold (**denature**) a protein, which is why a boiled egg white turns solid and doesn't go back. And as the final project shows, one wrong amino acid can change a protein's behavior.

How many possible sequences are there? For a chain of 100 amino acids:

$$
20^{100} = 10^{100 \log_{10} 20} \approx 10^{130}
$$

far more than the number of atoms in the observable universe (about $10^{80}$). Life uses a tiny fraction of the possible proteins.

### Nucleic acids

**DNA** and **RNA** are chains of **nucleotides**, each made of a sugar, a phosphate and a **base**: A, G, C and T in DNA; A, G, C and U in RNA. The sequence of bases carries the genetic information. The genetics chapter covers them in detail.
