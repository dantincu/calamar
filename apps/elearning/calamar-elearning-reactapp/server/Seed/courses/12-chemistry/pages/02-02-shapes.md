# Molecular shapes and intermolecular forces

## VSEPR: electron pairs repel

The shape of a molecule follows from a simple idea, the **valence shell electron pair repulsion** (VSEPR) model: the electron groups around a central atom (bonds and lone pairs) repel each other and spread as far apart as possible. A double or triple bond counts as one group.

| Electron groups | Arrangement | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{BeCl}_2$ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 120° | $\text{BF}_3$, $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\text{CH}_4$ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° | $\text{PCl}_5$ |
| 6 | octahedral | 90° | $\text{SF}_6$ |

The tetrahedral angle comes from geometry. Put the central atom at the center of a cube and four atoms at alternate corners, such as $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,-1,-1)$. The angle $\theta$ between them satisfies:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{(1,1,1)\cdot(1,-1,-1)}{|(1,1,1)|\,|(1,-1,-1)|} = \frac{1 - 1 - 1}{3} = -\frac{1}{3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta \approx 109.47°
$$

### Lone pairs change the shape

Lone pairs take part in the arrangement but are invisible in the shape, which describes only the atoms. They also repel more than bonding pairs, squeezing the bond angles:

| Molecule | Groups | Lone pairs | Shape | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{CH}_4$ | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| $\text{NH}_3$ | 4 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | 107° |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | 4 | 2 | bent | 104.5° |

## Polar molecules

A molecule is **polar** if its bond polarities don't cancel. It depends on both bonds and shape:

- $\text{CO}_2$ has two polar C=O bonds, but the molecule is linear, so they cancel: **non-polar**;
- $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ has two polar O–H bonds at an angle of 104.5°: they add up, and water is strongly **polar**;
- $\text{CCl}_4$ is tetrahedral and symmetric: non-polar. $\text{CHCl}_3$ (chloroform) isn't symmetric: polar.

## Intermolecular forces

The bonds *within* a molecule are strong; the attractions *between* molecules are much weaker, but they decide melting and boiling points, solubility, and much of biology. From the weakest:

1. **London dispersion forces**: the electrons of any molecule fluctuate, creating momentary dipoles that attract neighbors. Present in all molecules, they grow with the number of electrons and the contact area. That is why the halogens go from gas ($\text{F}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$) to liquid ($\text{Br}_2$) to solid ($\text{I}_2$) going down the group.
2. **Dipole-dipole forces**: between polar molecules, the $\delta+$ end of one attracts the $\delta-$ end of another.
3. **Hydrogen bonds**: a strong special case, when hydrogen bonded to N, O or F (very electronegative, small atoms) is attracted to a lone pair on another N, O or F.

The effect of hydrogen bonds is striking. Compare the hydrides of group 16:

| Compound | Molar mass | Boiling point |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | 18 | 100 °C |
| $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ | 34 | −60 °C |
| $\text{H}_2\text{Se}$ | 81 | −41 °C |
| $\text{H}_2\text{Te}$ | 130 | −2 °C |

Going down the group, boiling points rise with size, as dispersion forces predict. Following that trend, water would boil around −80 °C or lower. Its hydrogen bonds keep it liquid at room temperature: without them, there would be no oceans, and no life as we know it.

Hydrogen bonds also hold together the two strands of DNA and shape proteins: strong enough to give structure, weak enough to come apart when a cell needs to read a gene.

## Like dissolves like

A substance dissolves well in a solvent with similar intermolecular forces. Polar and ionic substances (salt, sugar) dissolve in polar water; non-polar ones (oil, wax) dissolve in non-polar solvents (petrol, hexane). Oil and water don't mix because water molecules attract each other far more than they attract oil.

**Soap** bridges the two: its molecules have a charged head that likes water and a long non-polar tail that likes grease. The tails bury themselves in a drop of grease, the heads face the water, and the drop is carried away.
