# Solutions and concentration

A **solution** is a uniform mixture: a **solute** dissolved in a **solvent**. Most chemistry in labs and in living things happens in water solutions.

## Concentration

The most common measure in chemistry is **molarity** (molar concentration):

$$
c = \frac{n}{V} \qquad \text{in mol/L, written M}
$$

where $n$ is the moles of solute and $V$ the volume of the *solution* in liters. Other measures:

| Measure | Definition | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| molarity | mol of solute per L of solution | lab chemistry |
| mass percent | g of solute per 100 g of solution | food labels, commercial products |
| mass per volume | g per L (or mg per dL) | medicine, water quality |
| ppm | mg per kg (in water, about mg per L) | pollutants, trace elements |

**Example.** Physiological saline is 0.9 % NaCl: 9 g per liter. In molarity:

$$
c = \frac{9\ \text{g} / 58.44\ \text{g/mol}}{1\ \text{L}} = 0.154\ \text{M}
$$

## Preparing a solution

To make 250 mL of 0.100 M copper sulfate from the pentahydrate ($M = 249.68$ g/mol):

1. moles needed: $n = cV = 0.100 \times 0.250 = 0.0250$ mol;
2. mass: $m = nM = 0.0250 \times 249.68 = 6.24$ g;
3. dissolve in less water, then fill a 250 mL volumetric flask to the mark.

Use the molar mass of the form you weigh: the five water molecules of the hydrate are part of the mass.

## Dilution

Adding solvent doesn't change the amount of solute, so:

$$
c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2
$$

To make 500 mL of 0.1 M HCl from 1 M stock: $V_1 = 0.1 \times 500 / 1 = 50$ mL of stock, made up to 500 mL. (Safety rule for strong acids: add the acid to the water, never water to concentrated acid, which can boil and spatter.)

## Solubility

The **solubility** of a substance is the most that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature. A solution at that limit is **saturated**.

- Most solids dissolve better in hot water: sugar's solubility roughly doubles from 0 °C to 100 °C. NaCl is an exception: about 36 g per 100 g of water, almost independent of temperature.
- Gases dissolve **less** in warm water. That is why a warm soda goes flat faster, and why warm rivers hold less oxygen for fish.
- Gas solubility is proportional to its partial pressure (**Henry's law**): $c = k_H P$. A soda is bottled under CO₂ pressure; open it, and the CO₂ comes out of solution. Divers who rise too fast suffer the same effect with nitrogen in their blood ("the bends").

## Colligative properties

Some properties of a solution depend only on the **number** of dissolved particles, not on what they are:

- **Freezing point depression**: $\Delta T_f = i K_f b$, where $b$ is the molality (mol of solute per kg of solvent), $K_f = 1.86$ K·kg/mol for water, and $i$ is the number of particles per formula unit (2 for NaCl, which splits into two ions). Road salt melts ice this way.
- **Boiling point elevation**: $\Delta T_b = i K_b b$, with $K_b = 0.512$ K·kg/mol for water. Salting pasta water raises its boiling point only by a fraction of a degree: it is for flavor.
- **Osmotic pressure**: $\Pi = i c R T$. Water flows across a membrane toward the more concentrated side. This is why saline for drips is 0.9 %: it matches the blood, so cells neither swell nor shrink.

**Example.** How much does 1 L of sea water (about 35 g of salt per kg, mostly NaCl) freeze below pure water? $b = 35/58.44 = 0.60$ mol/kg, so $\Delta T_f \approx 2 \times 1.86 \times 0.60 \approx 2.2$ K. Sea water freezes at about −2 °C.
