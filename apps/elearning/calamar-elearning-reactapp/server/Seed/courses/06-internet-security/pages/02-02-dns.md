# DNS security

DNS turns names into addresses. If an attacker can control the answer, they can send you to their server, even when you typed the right name.

## How resolution works

Your device asks a **recursive resolver** (your ISP's, your company's, or a public one like 1.1.1.1 or 9.9.9.9). The resolver asks the root servers, then the `.example` servers, then `bank.example`'s authoritative servers, and caches the answer for its TTL.

Classic DNS uses **plain UDP on port 53**: no encryption and no authentication.

## Attacks

| Attack | How | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| **Eavesdropping** | anyone on the path sees every name you look up | DNS over HTTPS (DoH) or TLS (DoT) |
| **Spoofing and cache poisoning** | forged answers arrive before the real one, and get cached | random source ports and IDs, DNSSEC |
| **Resolver hijacking** | malware or a compromised router changes your DNS settings | protect routers, watch for changed settings |
| **Domain hijacking** | an attacker takes over your registrar account and changes your records | registrar MFA, registry lock |
| **Subdomain takeover** | `docs.example.com` still points (CNAME) to a deleted cloud resource, which an attacker re-registers | remove dangling records |
| **Typosquatting** | `examp1e.com`, `example-login.com` | register look-alikes, monitor new registrations |

## Why cache poisoning was hard, and became harder

To forge an answer, an off-path attacker must guess the query's 16-bit transaction ID, and (since 2008) its random UDP source port, about 16 more bits. The chance of one forged packet matching is about

$$
\frac{1}{2^{16} \times 2^{16}} = 2^{-32} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{-10}
$$

Before source-port randomization, it was $2^{-16}$, and the 2008 Kaminsky attack showed how to make unlimited attempts. Randomization raised the bar; **DNSSEC** removes the guessing game altogether.

## DNSSEC

DNSSEC signs DNS records. Resolvers that validate check the signatures up a chain of trust to the root, so a forged answer fails validation, however well-timed it is. It provides **integrity and authenticity, not confidentiality**. Enable it at your DNS provider if they support it (usually one click), and use a validating resolver.

## Encrypted DNS

- **DoH** (DNS over HTTPS, port 443) and **DoT** (DNS over TLS, port 853) encrypt queries between you and the resolver.
- Modern browsers and operating systems support them (Windows 11, Android's "Private DNS", Firefox, Chrome).
- The resolver still sees your queries, so choose one whose privacy policy you trust.

## E-mail and DNS

E-mail security relies on DNS records that you publish for your domain:

| Record | Says |
|---|---|
| **SPF** | which servers may send mail for my domain |
| **DKIM** | the public key that verifies my mail's signatures |
| **DMARC** | what receivers should do with mail that fails SPF/DKIM (`p=reject`), and where to send reports |

Without them, anyone can send e-mail that claims to come from your domain. Start DMARC at `p=none` to collect reports, then move to `quarantine` and `reject`.
