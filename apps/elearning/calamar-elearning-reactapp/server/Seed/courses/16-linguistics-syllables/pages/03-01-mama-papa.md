# Why mama and papa are everywhere

## The pattern

Look at the words for "mother" and "father" in some unrelated languages:

| Language | Family | Mother | Father |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mandarin | Sino-Tibetan | māma | bàba |
| Swahili | Niger-Congo | mama | baba |
| Tamil | Dravidian | ammā | appā |
| Korean | Koreanic | eomma | appa |
| Turkish | Turkic | anne | baba |
| Hungarian | Uralic | anya | apa |
| Indonesian | Austronesian | ibu, mama | bapak, papa |
| Russian | Indo-European | mama | papa |
| Romanian | Indo-European | mamă | tată |
| Hindi | Indo-European | mā̃, ammā | pitā, bāp |
| Arabic (colloquial) | Semitic | māmā, umm | bābā, ab |

These languages belong to families with no demonstrated relationship. Yet the words for mother keep using **nasals** ([m], [n]), and the words for father keep using **stops** ([p], [b], [t], [d]), in simple, often doubled syllables. The anthropologist George Murdock compared kinship words across hundreds of languages in 1959 and found the pattern far more often than chance would allow. In 1960 the linguist Roman Jakobson gave the classic explanation in an essay titled "Why 'Mama' and 'Papa'?".

## The explanation: babbling

At around 6 to 10 months, babies everywhere start **canonical babbling**: repeated consonant-vowel syllables like *bababa*, *mamama*, *dadada*. The first consonants are those easiest to make and see: lip sounds ([m], [b], [p]) and tongue-tip sounds ([n], [d], [t]). The first vowel is the open [a], made with the jaw down. So the first syllables a baby produces are *ma*, *pa*, *ba*, *na*, *da*, *ta*, which is exactly the inventory of the table.

Then the adults do the rest. Parents hear *mama* and *papa* as names for themselves, and repeat them back to the child, and the words become part of the language. The words are **made by children and adopted by adults**, again and again, in every generation, in every culture.

Why nasals for mothers? Jakobson suggested that the *m* sound is made with closed lips while air flows through the nose, the position of a nursing baby, and that *mmm* sounds often accompany feeding and wanting, and then become associated with the person who feeds. This part is a hypothesis, harder to test than the babbling, but the statistical pattern is solid.

## The exceptions prove the rule

The pattern is a strong tendency, not a law:

- In **Georgian**, *mama* means **father**, and mother is *deda*. The same easy syllables, assigned the other way round.
- **Japanese** native words are *haha* "mother" and *chichi* "father" (though children today often say *mama* and *papa*, borrowed from European languages).
- **Finnish** has *äiti* and *isä*.

These exceptions show that the words are not biologically fixed. The babbling supplies the syllables; each culture decides who gets which.

## Not inheritance

Couldn't *mama* simply be inherited from a very old ancestor of all languages? Linguists say no, for two reasons:

1. The words appear in families that show no other signs of common origin, and in all of them, they are exactly the kind of syllables babies make. A shared origin is not needed to explain them, so it can't be proven by them.
2. Nursery words are constantly **re-created**. Romanian shows this beautifully: Latin had *māter* "mother", but Romanian *mamă* continues Latin *mamma*, a nursery word, and the inherited *māter* was lost. Its father word, *tată*, comes from Latin *tata*, also a nursery word. Both words in Romanian are babble that won.

That is why historical linguists, when comparing languages to prove they are related, deliberately **exclude** words like *mama*, *papa*, and imitations of sounds: they are too likely to arise independently (chapter 4).

## Beyond parents

The same babbling syllables name other people close to small children: *nana* or *nan* (grandmother in English, and similar words in many languages), *tata* (Polish and Czech "dad", Romanian *tată*), *dada*, *baba* (in Russian and Polish, *baba* means "old woman" or "grandma"; in Hindi and Turkish, *baba* is a respectful word for father or an elder). They are among the most widely distributed words on Earth.
