# A world tour: ma, mi, na, ne, no

The syllables in this page start with a **nasal** consonant, [m] or [n], the easiest consonants for babies to make (chapter 3) and among the most common in the world's languages. Each section shows the dataset's entries, then the patterns worth noticing.

node syllables.js ma

- **Mother** appears in unrelated languages: Mandarin *mā*, Hindi *mā̃*, Bengali *ma*, Vietnamese *má*. That is not a coincidence, and not inheritance: chapter 3 explains it.
- **What**: Arabic *mā* and Hebrew *ma* are related: both are Semitic languages and inherited the word from their common ancestor. Arabic *mā* also negates verbs: *mā kataba*, "he did not write".
- **Horse** appears twice: Mandarin *mǎ* 马 and Vietnamese *mã*. Here the link is borrowing: the Sino-Vietnamese *mã* comes from Chinese (chapter 4).
- **Tone** splits *ma* five ways in Mandarin and six in Vietnamese, and Thai separates *come*, *horse* and *dog* by pitch alone.
- Italian *ma* "but" comes from Latin *magis* "more", the same word that gives Spanish *más* "more" and Romanian *mai* "more": three meanings of one ancestor.

```

```
"mi": 15 meanings in 11 languages

  Spanish     mi / mí      mi                    my / me (after a preposition)
  Italian     mi           mi                    me, to me (clitic)
  Romanian    mi           mi                    to me (clitic: mi-e foame, I am hungry)
  Mandarin    迷            mí (tone 2)           to be lost; a fan, enthusiast
              米            mǐ (tone 3)           rice (uncooked); meter
              蜜            mì (tone 4)           honey
  Japanese    身            mi                    body; oneself
              実            mi                    fruit, nut; substance
              見            mi-                   see (stem of miru)  [stem]
  Hungarian   mi           mi                    we; what
  Hebrew      מי           mi                    who
  Turkish     mi / mı / mu / mü mi                    yes-no question particle (follows vowel harmony)  [particle]
  Persian     می‌          mi-                   prefix of ongoing or habitual action (mi-ravam, I go / am going)  [prefix]
  Swahili     mi-          mi-                   plural prefix of noun class 4 (mti 'tree', miti 'trees')  [prefix]
  Vietnamese  mì           mì                    wheat noodles
```

- **Me and my**: Spanish *mi*, Italian *mi*, Romanian *mi*, all from Latin *mihi* / *meus*. The *m* of the first person is one of the most stable features of Indo-European: English *me*, *my*, *mine*, Russian *menya*, Persian *man* "I", Hindi *main* "I".
- **What and who**: Hungarian *mi* "what" and Hebrew *mi* "who": unrelated languages, a coincidence. But *m-* question words are frequent in many families: Turkish *mi* (a question particle), Finnish *mikä* "what", Hungarian *mi*. Linguists still debate whether that reflects very ancient relationships or chance.
- **Grammar**: Turkish *mi* turns a sentence into a yes-no question; Persian *mi-* marks ongoing action; Swahili *mi-* makes a plural (*mti* "tree", *miti* "trees"). Short, frequent syllables often become grammatical markers.

            那            nà (tone 4)           that
  Hindi     न / ना       na / nā               no, not
  Bengali   না           na                    no, not
  Persian   نه           na                    no
  Greek     να           na                    to (marks the subjunctive: 'thelo na pao', I want to go); here!  [particle]
  Russian   на           na                    on, onto; here, take it!
  Polish    na           na                    on, onto
  Korean    나            na                    I (informal)
  Japanese  名            na                    name
            な            na                    don't! (after a verb); sentence-final emphasis  [particle]
  Swahili   na           na                    and, with; (kuwa na) to have
  Arabic    ـنا          -nā                   us, our (suffix)  [suffix]
```

- **No** with *n* is widespread: Hindi *na*, Bengali *na*, Persian *na*, and (next section) Czech and Russian *ne*, Spanish *no*. In Indo-European this is inheritance: an ancient negation *\*ne*, still visible in English *no*, *not*, *never* and *none*.
- **On**: Russian and Polish *na*, from the shared Slavic preposition.
- The Greek *na* is a grammatical word: *thélo na páo*, "I want to go", literally "I want that I go".

## ne: yes or no?

```bash
node syllables.js ne
```

```
"ne": 11 meanings in 10 languages

  Greek     ναι          ne                    yes
  Korean    네            ne                    yes (polite)
  Czech     ne           ne                    no
  Russian   не           ne                    not
  Romanian  ne           ne                    us (clitic pronoun)
  French    ne / nez / né ne                    not (with pas) / nose / born  (nez and né are pronounced [ne], ne is [nə])
  Hindi     ने           ne                    marks the agent of a past transitive verb (ergative)  [particle]
  Japanese  ね            ne                    isn't it? right? (seeking agreement)  [particle]
            根            ne                    root
  Mandarin  呢            ne (neutral tone)     particle: 'and what about...?', ongoing action  [particle]
  Turkish   ne           ne                    what
```

The most confusing syllable in the world for travellers. In **Greek** (*ναι*) and **Korean** (*네*), *ne* means **yes**. In **Czech** and many Slavic languages, *ne* means **no**, and in Russian *не* means *not*. To make things worse, Czech for *yes* is *ano*, which sounds to an English speaker like *no*, and Greek *no* is *óchi*. Greek *nai* and Korean *ne* are unrelated: a pure coincidence. Notice also Hindi *ne*, a grammatical particle that marks who did something in the past tense, and French *nez* "nose" and *né* "born".

Japanese *no* is one of its most frequent words: *watashi no hon*, "my book" (literally "I-*no* book"). Russian *no* means "but", and Czech *no* is a casual "yeah" or "well": yet another trap for the Czech-English yes-no pair.

## What the tour shows

Three different reasons make the same syllable mean similar things in different languages:

1. **Inheritance** from a common ancestor: Spanish, Italian and Romanian *mi*; Arabic and Hebrew *ma*; the *n* negations of Indo-European.
2. **Borrowing**: Vietnamese *mã* "horse" from Chinese.
3. **Shared human tendencies**: *ma* "mother" in unrelated languages.

And everything else is **coincidence**: Greek and Korean *ne* "yes", Hungarian and Hebrew *mi*. Telling these apart is the work of historical linguistics (chapter 4).
