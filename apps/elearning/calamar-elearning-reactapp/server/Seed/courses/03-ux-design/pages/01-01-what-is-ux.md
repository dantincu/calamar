# What is UX design?

**User experience (UX)** is everything a person perceives and feels while using a product: whether it does what they need, how easily they get it done and how they feel along the way. **UX design** is the deliberate shaping of that experience.

## UX vs UI

| | UX | UI |
|---|---|---|
| Question | *Does this solve the right problem, in a way people can follow?* | *What does each screen look like, and how does each control behave?* |
| Output | research findings, flows, wireframes, prototypes, test results | visual design, components, a design system |
| Fails when | a beautiful app nobody needs | a useful app nobody can figure out |

They overlap, and on small teams one person does both. This course covers both, because color is where they meet: a palette is a visual decision with usability consequences (contrast, meaning, accessibility).

## The process: the double diamond

The UK Design Council's *double diamond* describes two rounds of widening and narrowing:

1. **Discover** (widen): research the problem. Talk to users, watch them work, read support tickets.
2. **Define** (narrow): decide which problem you're solving, written as a clear problem statement.
3. **Develop** (widen): explore many solutions with sketches, wireframes and prototypes.
4. **Deliver** (narrow): test, refine and ship one of them.

In practice you loop: a test in *Deliver* often sends you back to *Define*.

## Usability, measured

ISO 9241-11 defines usability as the extent to which specified users can achieve specified goals with **effectiveness** (did they succeed?), **efficiency** (how much effort did it take?) and **satisfaction** (how did it feel?). All three can be measured, and chapter 5 shows how.

## Accessibility is part of UX

About one person in six lives with a significant disability, and everybody is temporarily impaired sometimes: sunlight on a phone, a broken arm, a noisy train. Accessible design (enough contrast, keyboard support, clear language) is better design for everyone. The color chapter treats the WCAG contrast rules as hard requirements, not afterthoughts.
