# DNA and its replication

## The structure

**DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a double helix, worked out in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick, using X-ray images by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. Each strand is a chain of nucleotides: a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate, and one of four **bases**: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C).

- The sugars and phosphates form the **backbone** on the outside.
- The bases point inwards and pair across the two strands: **A with T** (two hydrogen bonds) and **G with C** (three).
- The strands run in **opposite directions** (antiparallel), labeled 5′ and 3′ after the carbon atoms of the sugar.

Base pairing means that each strand determines the other. If one strand reads `5'-ATGGTGCAC-3'`, the other must read `3'-TACCACGTG-5'`. It also explains **Chargaff's rules**, measured before the structure was known: in any DNA, the amount of A equals the amount of T, and G equals C.

### Some numbers

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| distance between base pairs | 0.34 nm |
| base pairs per turn of the helix | about 10.5 |
| width of the helix | 2 nm |
| human genome (one set) | about 3.1 billion base pairs |
| protein-coding genes | about 20,000 |
| fraction of the genome that codes for protein | about 1.5 % |

Each of your cells holds two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent, so about $6.2 \times 10^9$ base pairs. Laid end to end:

$$
6.2 \times 10^9 \times 0.34 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} \approx 2.1\ \text{m}
$$

of DNA packed into a nucleus about 6 µm across. It is wound around proteins called **histones**, then coiled and folded again, into the 46 chromosomes visible when a cell divides.

**GC content**, the fraction of G and C bases, varies between species and between regions of a genome. GC pairs have three hydrogen bonds instead of two, so GC-rich DNA needs more heat to separate its strands: a fact used every day in PCR.

## Replication

Watson and Crick noted that the pairing "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism". Replication is **semi-conservative**: the strands separate, and each serves as a template for a new partner, so each daughter molecule keeps one old strand. (Meselson and Stahl proved this in 1958 by growing bacteria with heavy nitrogen-15 and then normal nitrogen-14, and weighing the DNA.)

The main actors:

1. **Helicase** unwinds the helix, opening a **replication fork**.
2. **Primase** lays down a short RNA primer.
3. **DNA polymerase** adds nucleotides that pair with the template, always building in the 5′→3′ direction. So one strand (the *leading* strand) is made continuously, the other (the *lagging* strand) in short pieces that **ligase** joins.

### Speed and accuracy

A bacterial polymerase adds about 1,000 nucleotides per second; human polymerases are slower (about 50 per second), so a human chromosome is copied from thousands of starting points at once.

The polymerase makes about one error in $10^5$ bases, but it proofreads its work, and a separate **mismatch repair** system checks again. The final error rate is about one in $10^9$ to $10^{10}$ bases per division.

Even so, some changes slip through. The human germline mutation rate is about $1.2 \times 10^{-8}$ per base per generation. Each child gets about $6.2 \times 10^9$ bases from the parents, so:

$$
6.2 \times 10^9 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-8} \approx 74
$$

new mutations, measured in sequencing studies at roughly 60–70. Most fall outside genes and have no effect. These mutations are the raw material of evolution.

## Copying DNA in the lab: PCR

The **polymerase chain reaction** (Kary Mullis, 1983) copies a chosen stretch of DNA by repeating three temperatures:

1. about 95 °C: separate the strands;
2. about 55–65 °C: short **primers** bind on each side of the target;
3. about 72 °C: a heat-resistant polymerase (from the hot-spring bacterium *Thermus aquaticus*) copies the target.

Each cycle doubles the target, so after $n$ cycles one molecule becomes about $2^n$: $2^{30} \approx 10^9$ copies in an hour or two. It is the basis of COVID tests, forensic DNA profiling, and the DNA sequencing that the next pages rely on.
