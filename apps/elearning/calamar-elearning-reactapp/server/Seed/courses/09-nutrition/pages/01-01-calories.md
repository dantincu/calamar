# Calories and energy balance

## What a calorie is

A **kilocalorie** (kcal, the "calorie" on food labels) is the energy that heats 1 kg of water by 1 °C. The SI unit is the joule, and EU labels show both:

$$
1\ \text{kcal} = 4.184\ \text{kJ}
$$

So a label that says *1 891 kJ / 452 kcal* gives the same amount twice: $1891 / 4.184 \approx 452$.

## Where the energy comes from

The body gets energy from four things in food. The EU uses these conversion factors (Regulation 1169/2011), the **Atwater factors**:

| Nutrient | kcal per gram |
|---|---|
| Fat | 9 |
| Alcohol | 7 |
| Protein | 4 |
| Carbohydrate | 4 |
| Fiber | 2 |

Water, vitamins and minerals provide no energy. A food's energy is therefore

$$
E = 9F + 4C + 4P + 2\,\text{Fib} + 7A
$$

with each nutrient in grams per 100 g. Here $C$ is the *available* carbohydrate, as on EU labels, where fiber is listed separately. (US tables include fiber in "carbohydrate", so subtract it first.)

For example, 100 g of dry lentils has 24.6 g protein, 1.1 g fat, 10.7 g fiber, and $63.4 - 10.7 = 52.7$ g of available carbohydrate:

$$
E = 9(1.1) + 4(52.7) + 4(24.6) + 2(10.7) = 9.9 + 210.8 + 98.4 + 21.4 \approx 341\ \text{kcal}
$$

Food tables list 352 kcal, because they use more precise factors for each food. The general factors are an approximation, but a good one: usually within a few percent.

### Energy density

Fat has more than twice the energy of carbohydrate per gram, and water has none. That is why **energy density** (kcal per gram) varies so much:

| Food | kcal / 100 g |
|---|---|
| cabbage | 25 |
| apple | 52 |
| boiled potatoes | ≈ 87 |
| cooked lentils | ≈ 116 |
| white bread | 265 |
| telemea cheese | 264 |
| biscuits | ≈ 450 |
| walnuts | 654 |
| sunflower oil | 884 |

One tablespoon of oil (about 10 g) has as much energy as 350 g of cabbage. Foods with low energy density (vegetables, fruit, soups, legumes) fill you up for fewer calories, which is one of the most reliable findings in appetite research.

## Energy balance

Body weight changes when intake and expenditure differ:

$$
\Delta\text{stored energy} = E_{\text{in}} - E_{\text{out}}
$$

This is the first law of thermodynamics, and it always holds. What it doesn't say is that both sides **respond** to each other. Eat less and the body burns less, both because a lighter body needs less energy and because of adaptation. That is why weight loss slows down over time (see *Weight change in practice*).

$E_{\text{out}}$ has three parts:

- **Resting metabolism** (BMR), about 60–70 %: keeping you alive at rest.
- **The thermic effect of food**, about 10 %: digesting. It is highest for protein (20–30 % of its energy) and lowest for fat (0–3 %).
- **Physical activity**, the rest: from about 15 % for a desk worker to over 40 % for an athlete. It includes everyday movement such as walking, standing and fidgeting, which varies a lot between people.

## Quick check

1. A drink has 0 g fat, 10.6 g carbohydrate and 0 g protein per 100 ml. How many kcal in a 330 ml can? *(About 140 kcal: $4 \times 10.6 \times 3.3$.)*
2. Why does 100 g of walnuts have more energy than 100 g of dry lentils, although both are "dry" foods? *(Fat: 65 g vs 1 g.)*
